Read the Case Study

Sharing Secrets to Tackle Slavery: Tony's Chocolonely and the Open Chain Model

Based on reporting from the Financial Times, November 2025

Tony's Chocolonely is a Dutch chocolate company founded in 2005 by journalist Teun ("Tony") van de Keuken after he investigated slavery and child labor in the cocoa industry. Unlike most businesses, Tony's defines success not by profit margins but by outcomes: child labor rates among partner farms, farmer living income benchmarks, and the strength of co-operative organizations. The company's stated mission is not to sell more chocolate bars — it is to end exploitation in the cocoa supply chain entirely.

The approach Tony's has taken to pursue that mission is unconventional. Rather than treating its ethical sourcing practices as proprietary assets, the company has built an "Open Chain" platform that makes its entire supply chain framework available for competitors to adopt. The Open Chain model rests on five core principles: traceable cocoa beans mapped via GPS; prices above the Fairtrade premium to move farmers toward a living income; investment in strong farmer co-operatives covering training, governance, and bulk purchasing; long-term sourcing relationships of at least five years; and programs to improve quality and yields on farms. The platform includes contract templates, a BeanTracker data tool, and shared protocols giving all supply chain actors — from co-operatives to processors — real-time visibility into bean volumes, locations, and payments.

This model has attracted a growing group of "Mission Allies," including Ben & Jerry's, Aldi, and several smaller food companies. In the 2023/24 season, 20 companies sourced more than 17,000 tonnes of cocoa through the platform — a year-over-year increase of nearly 20 percent. Among Tony's longest-standing co-operative partners, the child labor rate stands at approximately 3.9 percent, compared with an industry average of nearly 50 percent.

Tony's is candid about the limits of its model. The company acknowledges it cannot guarantee every bar is entirely free of child labor, and that its ambition — to scale impact without compromising mission — remains financially unresolved. In 2023, revenues grew 23 percent to €150 million, but Tony's posted a €2.7 million loss, partly attributable to the higher premiums it pays to farmers and continued investment in global expansion. The broader cocoa market faces mounting pressure: rising input costs, crop disease, and climate instability have reduced yields across Ghana and Ivory Coast.

Tony's approach is not unique in spirit. Footwear company Allbirds open-sourced its proprietary green foam material, and Patagonia has established an open industry standard for regenerative organic certification. Yet sharing supply chain practices remains rare — such information is more typically treated as a competitive advantage. The core tension Tony's raises is whether protecting intellectual property or sharing it more broadly creates greater value for the company, for farmers, and for the industry as a whole.

Activity question you will research: How can Tony's Chocolonely scale its Open Chain model to drive industry-wide change in ethical cocoa sourcing, and what are the strategic trade-offs involved in sharing proprietary supply chain practices with competitors?

Brainstorm: What Information Would Be Useful?

Before searching a database, it helps to think clearly about what you actually need to know. This brainstorm will sharpen your research question and make your database searches more purposeful.

Your selections will appear here once you reach this step.

Company-specific data to consider

Think about what you would want to know specifically about the company you selected:

  • What is the company's current supply chain and sourcing strategy, and how has it evolved?
  • How has the company performed financially — are there signs that its sourcing model helps or hurts profitability?
  • What have company leaders said publicly about their approach to ethical sourcing or supply chain transparency?
  • Has the company faced legal scrutiny, NGO investigations, or reputational challenges related to labor standards?
  • What metrics does the company use to measure success — and how do those compare to industry norms?

Useful source types for company-level research include news articles, company annual and impact reports, earnings releases, and company press releases.

For each type of information, note what kind of source you would look for. Write in notes, bullet points, or full sentences. Please describe the company-specific information you would look for.

Industry-specific data to consider

Strong recommendations are grounded in context. Think about what you would need to know about the broader cocoa and agricultural supply chain industry:

  • What are the prevailing child labor and forced labor rates across cocoa-producing regions in West Africa?
  • How common are open-source or collaborative sustainability models in the food and commodity sectors?
  • What certification schemes exist — Fairtrade, Rainforest Alliance, UTZ — and how effective are they at improving farmer outcomes?
  • What economic pressures face cocoa farmers — price volatility, crop disease, climate change, land tenure?
  • How do major competitors compare on ethical sourcing commitments and supply chain transparency?

Useful source types for industry-level research include industry and market research reports, government and international labor data (such as ILO reports), and academic research on supply chain management, agricultural economics, or labor standards.

How Boolean search strings work

Most academic databases support Boolean operators. Use them to control the scope and precision of your results:

  • AND — narrows results; both terms must appear: supply chain transparency AND cocoa
  • OR — broadens results; useful for synonyms: child labor OR forced labor OR child labour
  • NOT — excludes a term: ethical sourcing NOT coffee
  • Quotation marks " " — searches an exact phrase: "living income"
  • Truncation * — finds all word forms: sustain* finds sustainable, sustainability, sustaining
  • Parentheses ( ) — groups synonyms: ("child labor" OR "child labour" OR "forced labor")

Building an effective search string

A well-constructed search string typically has two or three concept groups connected by AND, with synonyms inside each group connected by OR:

Concept 1 AND Concept 2 AND Concept 3 ↓ ↓ ↓ (term OR synonym) (term OR synonym) (term OR synonym)

Example search string:

"supply chain transparency" AND (cocoa OR chocolate) AND ("child labor" OR "child labour" OR "forced labor") AND ("Tony's Chocolonely" OR "fair trade" OR Fairtrade)

Tips for refining your search

  • Too many results? Add another AND concept, use more specific terms, or apply date and publication-type filters in the database's filter panel.
  • Too few results? Remove one AND concept, replace specific terms with broader ones, or add OR synonyms to an existing group.
  • Try subject headings: Many databases assign controlled vocabulary terms to articles. When you find a relevant article, check its subject headings and add those terms to future searches.
  • Search multiple databases: A business news database will return different results than an academic research database or an industry report database — both may be useful.
  • Separate your searches: Run one search focused on your company and a separate search on the broader industry or research strain. Combining everything in one string often narrows results too much.
Include synonyms, related concepts, important proper nouns, and alternative phrasings. Think about how different authors might describe the same idea. Please list your keywords before continuing.

Step 6 of 7

Understanding AI Use in Your Research

Before completing your worksheet, review the course AI policy. Understanding when AI is and isn't appropriate will help you use it as a genuine tool — without replacing the research and analysis that is yours to do.

The AI Research Workflow

AI can play a supporting role at certain stages, but the core work of researching, synthesizing, and writing must be your own.

YOU

1. Research

Search databases, reputable news sources, and company websites. AI cannot do this step reliably.

YOU & AI

2. Understand & Organize

Use AI to clarify concepts or terminology, and to help organize your own notes and research.

YOU

3. Apply Course Concepts

Synthesize research, connect findings to course frameworks, and develop your original argument.

YOU & AI

4. Write & Cite

Write in your own words and cite all sources. AI may review writing for grammar and clarity only.

Appropriate vs. Inappropriate Use

✓ Appropriate Use

  • Understanding course concepts
  • Learning research terminology
  • Organizing your own thoughts and notes
  • Brainstorming analysis angles
  • Clarifying financial or theoretical concepts
  • Reviewing your own writing for grammar and clarity

✗ Inappropriate Use

  • Asking AI for company-specific data — it will fabricate figures or use outdated information
  • Having AI generate your recommendations without your own analysis
  • Using AI to write your case analysis without your own synthesis
  • Asking AI to "do research" instead of searching databases or reputable sources
  • Using AI to summarize articles or sources in place of reading them yourself
